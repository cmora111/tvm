2 Engineering Library

The Engineering Library preserves the engineering knowledge behind The TermForge Project.

Source code explains what the software does.

Documentation explains how to use it.

The Engineering Library explains why it was designed that way.

This directory exists to preserve architectural decisions, research, experiments, discussions, lessons learned, and the story of TermForge as it evolves.

2.1 Collections

  • Journal — The engineering story of the project.
  • Architecture — Current and future system design.
  • ADR — Architecture Decision Records.
  • Research — Investigations and experiments.
  • History — Timeline, releases, milestones, and conversations.
  • Roadmap — Future direction.
  • Forge-Sessions — Engineering discussions and decisions.
  • Notes — Important discoveries and lessons learned.
  • Constitution — Core beliefs of the project.
  • Principles — Engineering principles that guide development.

2.2 Guiding Idea

The code explains what.

3 Engineering Knowledge Framework Incubator

The Incubator is the proving ground for the Engineering Knowledge Framework (EKF).

EKF is being discovered and validated through real engineering practice before it is extracted into an independent project. The collections here contain framework-owned knowledge rather than project-specific engineering records.

3.2 Incubation Principle

Patterns are discovered through practice before they are standardized.

Markdown is the authoritative source format for EKF artifacts. PDF, DOCX, EPUB, HTML, websites, and printed books are generated publishing products.

3.6 Graduation

A component may graduate only after it has demonstrated practical value, remained project-independent, and matured enough to serve other engineering projects.

4 Foundation

4.1 Purpose

The Foundation contains the philosophical and constitutional artifacts of the Engineering Knowledge Framework (EKF).

These artifacts define why the framework exists, what it believes, and the principles upon which it is built.

Unlike standards, templates, or implementation guidance, the Foundation is intentionally small and changes very infrequently. Every artifact within this collection is considered foundational to the framework and should remain stable over time.

The Foundation exists to provide a common understanding that guides every engineering decision made throughout the rest of the framework.

5 Design Philosophy

The Foundation answers the questions that every engineering methodology must answer before defining processes or standards.

  • Why does the framework exist?
  • What is its purpose?
  • What values guide its design?
  • What principles should never change?

These questions are answered before discussing implementation, standards, templates, or project-specific practices.

7 What Belongs Here

The Foundation contains only artifacts that define the identity and philosophy of the Engineering Knowledge Framework.

Typical artifacts include:

  • Foundational declarations
  • Constitutional documents
  • Mission and vision statements
  • Enduring engineering laws
  • Core philosophical concepts

These artifacts should remain project-independent and applicable to every implementation of the framework.

8 What Does Not Belong Here

The Foundation does not contain implementation details.

The following belong elsewhere within the framework:

  • Engineering standards
  • Templates
  • Architecture documentation
  • Research notes
  • Project journals
  • Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)
  • Roadmaps
  • Project-specific documentation

Those artifacts build upon the Foundation but never define it.

9 Relationship to the Framework

The Foundation is the bedrock of the Engineering Knowledge Framework.

Every other collection derives its purpose from the Foundation.

As the framework evolves, implementation artifacts may change.

The Foundation should change only with great care and only when the long-term philosophy of the framework itself evolves.

# Why EKF Exists
## A Declaration
Every engineering project creates more than source code.
It creates ideas.
Questions.
Experiments.
Discoveries.
Trade-offs.
Failures.
Breakthroughs.
Architectural decisions.
Over time, these become the project’s engineering knowledge.
Unfortunately, that knowledge is often scattered across emails, issue trackers, chat conversations, notebooks, whiteboards, and the memories of the engineers who created it. As projects evolve and contributors change, much of that knowledge is gradually lost.
The Engineering Knowledge Framework (EKF) exists to change that.
EKF provides a practical, modular methodology for capturing, organizing, preserving, and communicating engineering knowledge throughout the lifetime of a project.
Its purpose is not to replace engineering.
Its purpose is to preserve the reasoning that engineering produces.
EKF recognizes engineering knowledge as a first-class engineering artifact deserving the same discipline, craftsmanship, review, and long-term care as source code itself.
The framework is intentionally project-agnostic.
It is designed to support projects of every size and discipline, allowing each project to adopt only the components that provide value while maintaining a common philosophy for preserving engineering knowledge.
EKF was not designed in isolation.
It emerged through real engineering practice while building real software.
Every concept within the framework is expected to demonstrate practical value before becoming part of the framework itself.
The framework serves engineering projects.
Engineering projects do not exist to serve the framework.
As projects grow, they refine the framework.
As the framework matures, it helps future projects preserve their knowledge more effectively.
This continuous feedback creates an engineering ecosystem in which experience becomes understanding, understanding becomes knowledge, and knowledge becomes lasting engineering wisdom.
EKF exists so that future engineers inherit more than source code.
They inherit understanding.

Engineering knowledge deserves the same discipline as engineering code.

13 Engineering Knowledge Lifecycle

13.1 Opening Statement

Engineering knowledge emerges through observation, investigation, experience, and honest inspection.

It is not created in a single moment, nor is it acquired simply by reading documentation or implementing a solution. Engineering knowledge develops through deliberate inquiry, practical experience, thoughtful reflection, and the willingness to continually inspect both successes and failures.

The Engineering Knowledge Lifecycle describes how engineering knowledge matures from an initial observation into understanding that can be preserved, refined, and shared with future engineers.

13.2 Purpose

The purpose of the Engineering Knowledge Lifecycle is to provide a common model for understanding how engineering knowledge is developed, validated, and preserved.

Rather than focusing on documents or processes, the lifecycle focuses on the progression of engineering thought. Documents, standards, architecture decisions, and software implementations are artifacts produced during that journey; they are not the journey itself.

By understanding this lifecycle, engineers gain a framework for recognizing where knowledge originates, how it matures, and when it has earned the confidence to become part of the Engineering Knowledge Framework.

13.3 The Lifecycle

Engineering knowledge develops through a deliberate progression.

  1. Observation

    • Recognizing something worthy of attention.

  2. Investigation

    • Exploring observations through research, experimentation, discussion, and analysis.

  3. Experience

    • Applying ideas in practice and learning from their outcomes.

  4. Honest Inspection

    • Critically evaluating both successes and failures without attachment to previous assumptions.

  5. Engineering Knowledge

    • Knowledge emerges when repeated observation, investigation, experience, and honest inspection produce understanding that has proven worthy of preservation.

The lifecycle is iterative rather than linear. New observations may arise at any stage, beginning the cycle anew and continually refining existing knowledge.

13.4 Relationship to EKF

The Engineering Knowledge Framework does not create engineering knowledge.

It preserves engineering knowledge that has emerged through disciplined engineering practice.

Research, discussions, architecture decisions, implementations, standards, principles, and the Forge Ledger each represent different points within this lifecycle. Together they provide a structured means of preserving engineering knowledge so that future engineers may inspect, refine, and extend it.

13.5 Guiding Principle

Engineering knowledge is not inherited.

It is earned.

The responsibility of EKF is to ensure that knowledge, once earned, is never unnecessarily lost.

15 The Constitution

15.1 The Foundational Principles of the Engineering Knowledge Framework

15.1.1 Preamble

Engineering has always been more than the creation of systems.

It is the pursuit of understanding.

Every successful project is built upon thousands of decisions—some large, some small. Every experiment, every design discussion, every alternative considered, every lesson learned, and every refinement contributes to the engineering story.

Yet too often, only the final result survives.

Source code is preserved.

Specifications are archived.

Releases are published.

The reasoning that gave those artifacts meaning is frequently lost.

The Engineering Knowledge Framework was established from a simple conviction:

Engineering knowledge is a first-class engineering asset.

Knowledge deserves to be preserved with the same discipline, care, and craftsmanship as the systems it helps create.

This Constitution establishes the enduring principles upon which the Engineering Knowledge Framework is built. It defines the philosophy that guides the framework, while allowing its methods, standards, and practices to evolve through experience.

The purpose of this framework is not merely to document engineering.

Its purpose is to preserve understanding.

For the engineer working today.

For the engineer who joins tomorrow.

And for every engineer who will one day inherit the work we leave behind.

17 Article I — Engineering Knowledge

Field Value
Status Draft
Collection Constitution
Version 0.1
Primary Question What is engineering knowledge?

17.1 Purpose

This Article defines engineering knowledge and establishes it as an engineering asset worthy of deliberate stewardship.

It provides the constitutional foundation upon which the Engineering Knowledge Framework is built.

17.2 Declaration

Engineering knowledge is the accumulated understanding that enables engineers to make informed decisions throughout the lifecycle of an engineered system.

Engineering knowledge extends beyond source code, specifications, and completed designs.

It includes the reasoning, observations, assumptions, experiments, alternatives, experience, and lessons that give engineering artifacts their meaning.

Engineering knowledge is created wherever engineers investigate problems, evaluate alternatives, make decisions, validate outcomes, communicate discoveries, and refine understanding through experience.

Because engineering knowledge directly influences the quality, maintainability, safety, and evolution of engineered systems, it shall be regarded as an engineering asset worthy of deliberate stewardship.

17.3 Constitutional Effect

This Article establishes engineering knowledge as the central concern of the Engineering Knowledge Framework.

All framework components, standards, templates, practices, and future constitutional articles shall support the discovery, organization, preservation, communication, and responsible stewardship of engineering knowledge.

17.4 Relationship to Future Articles

This Article defines what engineering knowledge is.

Subsequent Articles establish:

  • the responsibility for preserving engineering knowledge,
  • the purpose of the Engineering Knowledge Framework,
  • the scope of the framework,
  • and the principles governing its continued evolution.

Nothing in a subsequent Article may contradict the definition established here.

17.5 Engineering Principle

Engineering does not conclude when a system is completed.

Engineering continues wherever understanding is preserved, shared, and used to make better decisions.

17.6 Review Status

This Article is currently in the Draft stage.

It shall remain under review until it has completed a cooling period, undergone architectural inspection, and been formally approved as a Foundation artifact.

Approval of this Article shall be recorded in the Forge Ledger.

i# ⚒️ Forge Ledger

22.4 Purpose

The Forge Ledger preserves the enduring engineering principles discovered while applying the Engineering Knowledge Framework.

Unlike a project history, milestone log, or engineering journal, the Forge Ledger records the principles, insights, and truths uncovered through honest inspection and deliberate craftsmanship.

Each entry, known as a Strike, captures a lesson worthy of preservation—an idea that shaped the philosophy of the project and is expected to remain valuable long after individual features, implementations, or technologies have changed.

A Strike is not created because work was completed.

A Strike is created because understanding was gained.

The Forge Ledger exists to ensure that the wisdom earned at the anvil is never lost.

22.5 What Belongs in the Forge Ledger

A Strike records discoveries such as:

  • An engineering principle that changes how future decisions are made.
  • A philosophy uncovered through experience.
  • A lesson learned through honest inspection.
  • A timeless observation that strengthens the craft.

22.6 What Does Not Belong in the Forge Ledger

The Forge Ledger is not a changelog, release history, milestone tracker, or project journal.

Those artifacts record what happened.

The Forge Ledger records what was learned.

Every Strike represents understanding earned through craftsmanship.
